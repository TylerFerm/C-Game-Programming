Exercise 01
Vector Math Library
Vectors — Subtraction, Addition, Length, Normalization, Scalar Multiplication
Beginner
Why This Matters

Every single position, velocity, direction, and force in a game engine is a vector. The book's Math.h provides these operations — but building them yourself cements the math. You'll implement the exact functions the Asteroids project calls dozens of times per frame.

A vector has no fixed position — it's just a magnitude and direction. Two vectors with the same components are equal, no matter where they're drawn.

🚀

Engine Connection

In MoveComponent::Update(), the engine calls GetForward() * mForwardSpeed * deltaTime every frame to advance an actor. Every piece of that — the forward vector, the scalar multiply, the addition to position — is what you're building here.

Exercise 02
Angles & Steering
Angle↔Vector Conversion · atan2 · Enemy Facing
Beginner
Why This Matters

In the book's engine, every Actor stores its orientation as a single float mRotation in radians. But movement code needs a direction vector. These two conversions — angle-to-vector and vector-to-angle — are called constantly in every game.

The GetForward() function uses the unit circle to derive a direction from an angle. atan2 goes the other way: given a vector, what angle is that? Together, they let you point actors toward targets — exactly what the book shows with ship-facing-asteroid.

🎮

Engine Connection

The book's Actor::GetForward() does exactly what Task 1 asks. The atan2 steering is the code shown directly in the "Converting a Forward Vector to an Angle" section — you're reimplementing it console-testable, without needing SDL.

💡 Hint — SDL Coordinate System

SDL's screen has (0,0) at the top-left and +y going down. But the unit circle has +y going up. That's why you negate y in both conversions. If you skip this, ships face the mirror-y direction in the actual game.

Exercise 03
Dot & Cross Products
Angle Detection · Field of View · Triangle Normals
Intermediate
Why This Matters

The dot product tells you the angle between two vectors without trigonometry. In games it's used for: is an enemy in the player's field of view? Is a surface facing the light? Should a door open (is the player approaching from the front)?

The cross product finds a vector perpendicular to two others — essential for computing surface normals, which determine how light reflects off geometry in 3D games (used heavily from Chapter 6 onward).

🔦

Engine Connection

The book shows dot product for angle-between-vectors and cross product for triangle normals (Figure 3.10). Your field-of-view guard check is a direct use of dot product that you'll see in AI enemy systems, and the normal calculation is used in every lighting shader from Chapter 6 onward.

💡 Hint — Dot Product Signs

A positive dot product means the vectors point in roughly the same direction. Zero means perpendicular. Negative means they point away from each other. This is why if (Dot(forward, toEnemy) > 0) is a fast "is the enemy in front of me?" check — no trig needed.

Exercise 05
Physics & Circle Collision
Euler Integration · Forces & Mass · Circle-vs-Circle Detection
Advanced
Why This Matters

Real-world movement — jumping, gravity, bouncing — requires physics, not just velocity. Newton's second law (F = ma) lets you apply forces to objects and derive their movement. Euler integration is the simple, practical way games compute this each frame.

Circle collision is the cheapest possible collision test — and it's exactly what the Asteroids project uses for lasers hitting asteroids. The trick of comparing squared distances avoids a slow sqrt call every frame.

💥

💡 Hint — Why Squared Distance?

Computing sqrt is slow — it requires many CPU cycles. For collision testing where you just need to know if something hit, you can square both sides of the comparison: instead of distance <= radiiSum, check distanceSquared <= radiiSumSquared. Same answer, much faster. The book explicitly calls this out, and it's a technique you'll use everywhere.

💡 Hint — Force vs Impulse

A constant force (like gravity) gets added every frame. An impulse (like a jump) gets added only once. Because you clear mSumForces at the end of each Update, an impulse disappears after one frame automatically — which is exactly the right behavior.
